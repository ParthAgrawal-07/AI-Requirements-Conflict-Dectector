## Conflicting
The two requirements cannot both hold. Good options: a compromise wording that keeps what each
side needs, scoped by condition (e.g. "30 minutes generally, 5 minutes on payment screens"); or
"keep one, change the other", saying in the trade-off why one should give way. Each option
replaces the text of one requirement (`target`).

## Duplicate
The requirements say the same thing. Give one merged wording that keeps every detail either of
them states, and name the requirement to keep as `target`; the trade-off says what the other one
loses (its ID, its owner's wording).

## Ambiguous
The requirement can be read more than one way, or uses a word that cannot be tested. Give a
measurable rewrite: replace each vague word with a number or a testable criterion taken from the
text or the project context, or `[TBD — confirm]` when neither gives it.

## Incomplete
The requirement is missing a part: who, when, under what condition, how much, or what happens on
failure. Fill in the missing parts. Every value you could not take from the text or the context is
`[TBD — confirm]` — never a guess.

## Dependent
One requirement cannot be implemented or tested without the other. Options say which link to
record and the order to implement in, e.g. "Record that REQ-7 depends on REQ-3; implement REQ-3
first." `target` is null unless the option also rewrites a requirement to make the dependency
explicit.
